# Research notes: phone data for credit scoring

## Why we read these papers

Our project scores Thai thin-file applicants: people with fewer than 3 credit accounts in the bureau or less than 36 months of credit history ([data_spec.md](data_spec.md)). The bureau has little to say about them, so we plan to use prepaid top-up data from a partner mobile operator instead. The two papers in the Research tab help answer two questions: does phone data really predict who will repay, and which kinds of phone data can we legally use in Thailand? For each paper, this note explains what data was used, what the results were and why, and what transfers to Thailand under two sets of rules. The PDPA is Thailand's personal data protection law. Google Play's rules are Google's policies for apps in its Android app store; a Thai lender that offers a loan app there must follow them, or Google removes the app. Results are reported as AUC, which measures how well a model separates people who repay from people who do not: 0.5 is no better than a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect.

## R1: Óskarsdóttir et al. (2020), *The Value of Big Data for Credit Scoring*

### Data used

A bank and a mobile operator in the same country agreed to combine their data. The operator supplied five months of call-detail records, which are the logs operators keep for billing: one row per call, with the date, start time, length in seconds, and the (encrypted) numbers of the caller and the receiver. No call content is included. The bank supplied three things for its customers: basic details from the application (age, marital status, area of residence), debit account spending in the month before the credit card was issued, and credit card repayment records for the 12 months after. The study looked at about 22,000 people who received a new credit card during a three-month period in 2015. A person counted as a defaulter if they missed three or more payments in that first year, which is the standard Basel definition (roughly 90 days late); 4.5% of them did.

From the call records the authors built two kinds of features. The first kind describes the applicant's own calling habits: how many calls they made and received, how long the calls lasted, and how this was split between day and night and across days of the week (72 features). The second kind uses the call network. The authors drew everyone as a dot and every pair of people who had talked for more than five seconds in the three months before the card was issued as a line between two dots. They then marked the bank customers already known to be behind on their payments and asked, for each applicant, how close they sit to those people. The simplest version counts how many of an applicant's contacts are behind on payments. Two more advanced versions (called PageRank and spreading activation) let this "risk" flow outward along the lines, so that a friend of a friend of a late payer also receives a small amount. The idea rests on the observation that people tend to talk to people like themselves; the authors checked this and found that late payers were linked to good payers less often than chance would predict. In total each applicant had over 300 features.

### Results

The authors trained three types of model on eight different feature combinations. Logistic regression, the usual scorecard method, did poorly on every combination (AUC 0.52 to 0.61). The likely reason is that phone behaviour relates to default in a non-linear way: risk can change sharply above or below a certain level of calling, and logistic regression can only draw straight-line relationships unless the analyst bins the variables first. Random forests, which combine hundreds of decision trees and can capture such patterns, did far better, so the rest of the paper uses them. With bank data alone, the random forest reached AUC 0.90; adding all the phone features raised it to 0.92. The gain is small because the bank data already holds a lot of information about these people's finances, especially their spending. The more interesting result is that calling habits alone reached AUC 0.87, close to the bank model, which suggests that phone data can stand in for financial data when there is none. Network features alone were weaker (0.77 to 0.83), and among them the most useful signal was a positive one: having no risky contacts predicted that a person would repay.

The authors also measured profit, because a credit model's value depends on whom it approves, not only on how well it ranks people. They assumed a lender loses 80% of the balance when a customer defaults and earns 5% on a good customer, then picked the approval level that maximised profit for each model. The calling-habits-only model earned the most profit of all (about $619,000, against $614,000 for the bank-only model). The combined models ranked people slightly better but chose to reject more applicants, so they lost some good customers and earned less. Calling features also dominated the list of the most important features. A plausible explanation is that the way people use their phone reflects their daily routine, social stability and economic activity. The limits are that the data came from one country, covered only credit cards, and the applicants were already bank customers, so the phone data was not tested on people with no banking history at all.

### What transfers to Thailand

This paper supports the core idea of our project, with conditions. Google Play's rules do not block this design, because they only cover what a loan app may read from the customer's phone, and here the data already sits in the operator's systems. That is why our data spec treats phone data as fields the partner operator sends, not as data an app collects. The PDPA is the real constraint. The operator collected these records to provide phone service, not to support lending, so using them for credit scoring is a new purpose and sending them to a lender is a disclosure to another company. Both need the customer's clear consent, which means the application needs a consent screen that says what data goes to whom and why. The network features should be dropped altogether: they process data about friends and family who never agreed, and they would lower an applicant's score because of someone else's debts rather than their own behaviour. What we should request instead is applicant-level summaries computed by the operator, such as how often and how much a customer tops up and how long they have held the number, not lists of who called whom. Finally, the authors recommend using phone data only to help applicants, never to lower their score. This fits our goal of bringing thin-file applicants into credit; if phone data could also reduce scores, people who use their phones less, such as older or lower-income customers, would be penalised again. In a scorecard this can be done by setting any negative weight-of-evidence value for a phone variable to zero, so phone data can add points but never remove them.

## R2: Ots, Liiv and Tur (2020), *Mobile Phone Usage Data for Credit Scoring*

### Data used

An unnamed European consumer lender took loan applications through its own Android app. With the customer's permission, the app collected a wide range of data from the phone: device details, mobile network, calendar entries, the call log, the contact list, the list of installed apps, SMS details, the date and location of photos, and file information. The study covers 2,503 borrowers. The authors did not use phone numbers, calendar text or SMS text. From the rest they built 22 features, mostly monthly averages: ten about calls (numbers of incoming, outgoing, missed and unanswered calls, and average and longest call length), eight about SMS (numbers sent and received, from contacts or unknown numbers, and number of conversations), two about photos (photos per month and photos taken in different places), the number of contacts, and the Android version. A borrower counted as bad if they were 90 or more days late, which applied to 987 of them, or 39%. That bad rate is very high compared with R1's 4.5% and is typical of high-risk online consumer lending. The authors tried three ways of preparing the features (all 22; only those that passed a significance test; and those grouped into bins), five model types (logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, support vector machine and neural network), and three test-set sizes (10%, 25% and 40% of borrowers held back for testing).

### Results

Predictive power was weak. The best result was AUC 0.62, from a random forest on the 10% test set, but that test set held only about 250 people, so the number is unstable and partly luck. On the larger 40% test set (about 1,000 people), the best AUC was 0.58, and logistic regression never exceeded 0.56. Some set-ups fell below 0.50, which is worse than guessing. The accuracy figures tell the same story: the models were right about 60% of the time, which is what you would get by simply predicting that everyone will repay, since 61% did. Decision trees reached 100% accuracy on the training data but only about 56% on the test data, which means they memorised the training borrowers instead of learning general patterns (overfitting). There are several likely reasons for the weak result. The sample is small. The features measure how much someone uses their phone, which says little about income or money habits. Phone data was not combined with any financial data, unlike in R1. And there were no network features. The paper also contradicts itself: the results section names random forest as the best method, while the conclusion names the decision tree.

### What transfers to Thailand

Very little of this design can be used, because all of its data was read from the customer's phone by the loan app. Google Play bans personal loan apps from requesting access to contacts, photos and videos, files on the device, precise location, the phone number and the list of installed apps. This rule is often linked to loan apps that used borrowers' contacts and photos to shame them into repaying. A separate Google Play rule lets only the phone's default calling or SMS app read the call log and SMS, and a loan app is neither. Together these rules remove 21 of the 22 features; only the Android version is left, and it does not help with scoring. Google Play also requires Thai loan apps that charge 15% interest or more to show a licence from the Bank of Thailand or the Ministry of Finance, so a good model alone is not enough to launch an app. The PDPA leads to the same conclusion. A lender may collect only the data it needs, and counts of photos or contacts have no clear link to whether someone can repay. A contact list also contains other people's data, and a customer cannot consent on their behalf. Even if collecting this data were legal, the results show it would not be worth it. The lesson for our project confirms the plan in our data spec: if we want phone-based signals, we should get them from the operator, with consent, rather than by reading the customer's phone.

## Summary

R1 shows that phone data from an operator can predict default almost as well as bank data and can even produce higher profit, which is the evidence behind our project. R2 shows that data read directly from the handset predicts poorly, breaks Google Play's rules and conflicts with the PDPA. Our project should therefore request applicant-level top-up and usage summaries from a partner operator, collect explicit consent, use phone data only to raise scores, and avoid contact lists, photos, SMS, call-by-call records and network features.
